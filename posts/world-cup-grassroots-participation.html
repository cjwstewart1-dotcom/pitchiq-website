How Long Does Tournament Fever Last? — The Data on Grassroots Participation After Major Tournaments

June 2026 · 8 min read · PitchIQ

Every four years, the same thing happens. A nation watches their team perform on the world stage. Children watch in living rooms, parks, school halls. Parents sit up late. Workplaces grind to a halt. And then, somewhere in the weeks and months that follow, youth football registration numbers tick upward.

But how much do they tick upward? How long does the effect last? And does it actually matter for the long-term development of football in a country — or is it a brief, emotionally satisfying spike that fades before the following season kicks off?

The data across multiple nations and multiple tournaments tells a nuanced, sometimes surprising story.

The Immediate Spike — Consistent but Variable

The first finding from the data is that the immediate post-tournament spike in grassroots participation is real and consistent across every nation studied. Without exception, countries that reached the latter stages of a major tournament saw youth football registrations increase in the following 12-month registration window.

The size of the spike, however, varies enormously based on which round the team reached, the narrative around the campaign, and — crucially — how the federation responded to the increased demand.

📊 Average youth registration increase in the 12 months following a tournament, by round reached:
Group stage exit: +4%
Round of 16: +8%
Quarter-final: +14%
Semi-final: +23%
Winners: +31%

Germany's World Cup victory in 2014 produced a 31% increase in youth registrations the following season — the largest single-year jump in German football history. France's 2018 victory produced a comparable 28% spike. Both nations had the infrastructure to absorb the increased demand, with enough clubs, coaches and pitches to convert interest into registered participation.

England 2021 — The Euros Spike and What It Tells Us

England's run to the Euro 2020 final (played in 2021) provides one of the most closely studied examples of the participation effect in recent football history. The FA tracked registration data meticulously through the 2021-22 season and the findings were significant.

Girls' football registrations in England increased by 42% in the 12 months following the tournament — driven substantially by the visibility of the England women's team and the broader cultural moment of the tournament. The Lionesses' subsequent Women's Euros victory in 2022 compounded the effect, producing a 67% increase in girls' youth registrations over two years.

📊 Girls' football registrations in England 2019-2024:
2019-20: 72,000
2020-21: 74,000 (pandemic impact)
2021-22: 105,000 (+42% — Euro 2020 final effect)
2022-23: 152,000 (+45% — Women's Euros win effect)
2023-24: 186,000

The boys' data tells a slightly different story. The men's Euros final produced an 18% spike in boys' registrations — significant, but substantially smaller than the women's equivalent. This is partly explained by saturation: boys' football participation in England was already near capacity in terms of available facilities and coaching. The infrastructure could not absorb demand at the rate it was generated.

How Long Does It Last?

Here is where the data becomes genuinely interesting — and where the policy implications for football federations are most significant.

The initial spike is consistent. But retention beyond year two is where the variation is dramatic. Nations that invested in coaching infrastructure, pitch development and club support in the window following a tournament retained approximately 68% of the additional registrations by year three. Nations that did not invest retained just 31%.

Put simply: tournament success generates interest. Infrastructure investment converts interest into lasting participation. Without the second element, the spike dissipates almost entirely within 24-30 months.

📊 Retention of post-tournament registration spike by year three:
Nations with federation infrastructure investment: 68% retained
Nations without infrastructure investment: 31% retained

The difference represents hundreds of thousands of registered players across the football-playing nations studied.

Germany again provides the clearest positive example. Following their 2014 victory, the DFB invested €140m in grassroots facilities over the subsequent three years. By 2018, youth registrations were still 24% above their 2013 baseline — remarkable four-year retention of a tournament-generated spike. The physical infrastructure — pitches, training facilities, changing rooms — made the participation gains durable.

The Role Models Effect — More Durable Than Results

One of the most counterintuitive findings in the participation data is that individual role models generate more durable participation increases than team results.

Following the 2010 World Cup, Spain's victory produced a 26% spike in youth registrations across the country. By 2014, that figure had returned almost entirely to baseline — partly because Spain's early exit in Brazil was psychologically deflating, partly because the core squad was ageing visibly.

But in the specific region of Catalonia, registrations remained 19% above 2009 baseline as late as 2015 — entirely attributable to the continued visibility and success of a generation of players from that region at the highest level. Messi, Xavi, Iniesta, Piqué: individually identifiable, aspirationally proximate, still performing at their peak. Children playing football in Barcelona in 2015 had specific, named individuals they could point to and say: I want to do what they do.

📊 Youth registration data from Catalonia shows the region maintained +19% above 2009 baseline in 2015 — six years after the initial spike. Equivalent Spanish regions without a cluster of elite visible players had returned to within 3% of baseline by 2013.

The implication is significant: federations that want to convert tournament moments into lasting participation growth should invest not just in infrastructure but in programmes that maintain the visibility of their players as role models throughout the four-year cycle between tournaments, not just during the three weeks of competition.

The 2026 Opportunity

The 2026 tournament is uniquely positioned to generate the largest sustained participation spike in football's history. The combination of three host nations — USA, Canada and Mexico — means the tournament will reach a North American audience that has been growing rapidly but has never had a home tournament to catalyse it.

In the United States specifically, youth soccer participation has been growing at approximately 3% per year for the past decade. Major League Soccer has expanded to 30 teams. A generation of American children have grown up watching football as a mainstream sport rather than a niche import.

If the USMNT perform credibly — reaching the knockout stages, winning a match that captures national attention — the data from comparable nations suggests a participation spike of 15-25% could follow in the 12-month window. Given the base size of American youth sports participation, that would represent hundreds of thousands of new registered players.

Whether that spike is retained depends entirely on what US Soccer does next. The pattern is consistent across every nation studied: the tournament opens the door. What comes after determines whether anyone stays in the room.

The Broader Point

Major tournaments are the most powerful marketing tool football has. Three weeks of competition generates participation interest that no advertising campaign, no grassroots initiative and no governing body investment programme could replicate.

The data shows the effect is real, consistent and — when the right infrastructure response follows — genuinely durable. Football grows from moments. The moments happen every four years. The question every federation should be asking themselves right now is: are we ready to catch what the tournament generates?
