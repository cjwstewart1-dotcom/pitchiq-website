The xG Winners and Losers of the Group Stage

June 2026 · 7 min read · PitchIQ

The group stage is over. 48 teams became 32, and the scorelines told one story. But the expected goals data tells another — and in many cases, a very different one. Here's who got lucky, who got robbed, and what it means for the knockout rounds.

What xG Actually Tells Us

A quick reminder: xG measures the quality of chances created, not just the number of shots. A team can dominate possession, rack up attempts and still post a low xG if those attempts are poor quality. Conversely, a team that creates three clear-cut chances from in front of goal could have an xG of 2.1 despite only three shots.

When a team's actual goals scored significantly exceeds their xG, they've been clinical — or lucky. When it's significantly lower, they've either been wasteful or unfortunate. Over three group games, these patterns become meaningful.

The xG Overperformers — Fortune's Favourites

Germany are the most fascinating case study of the group stage. Their 7-1 demolition of Curaçao generated the kind of xG numbers that flatter any side. But strip that result out and look at their performances against Ecuador (a 2-1 defeat) and Paraguay (a 1-1 draw that ultimately ended in a penalty exit), and you see a team whose xG profile was deeply inconsistent. Germany created plenty — but they couldn't sustain it against teams of genuine quality. The Curaçao result masked fundamental attacking inefficiencies that cost them dearly.

Canada deserve mention here too. A 6-0 win over Qatar was staggering on the scoresheet, but Qatar's defensive fragility inflated every attacking metric Canada produced. Against stronger opposition they were more measured — which raises questions about how their xG numbers translate into the knockout rounds against sides who can actually defend.

The xG Underperformers — Unlucky or Just Wasteful?

Spain drew 0-0 with Cape Verde in what was the most striking xG mismatch of the group stage. Spain's style generates high-volume, high-quality chances almost by design — the tiki-taka pressing system ensures constant occupation of dangerous areas. A 0-0 against a side ranked significantly below them points clearly to either extraordinary goalkeeping or a conversion problem that should concern Luis de la Fuente heading into the last 16.

Netherlands finished their group with a 2-2 draw against Japan — a result that suggested balance, but the underlying data told a story of Dutch dominance that the scoreline didn't reward. They then drew 1-1 with Morocco in the Round of 16 and lost on penalties. A team that consistently generates high xG but cannot convert it is a team that will eventually run out of tournament.

Portugal drew 1-1 with DR Congo — a result that looks puzzling on paper for a side of their attacking quality. Their subsequent 5-0 win over Uzbekistan corrected the record somewhat, but the DR Congo draw is the kind of xG anomaly that suggests a team not yet at full throttle.

The Teams Whose xG Should Scare Everyone

France were the most ruthlessly efficient team of the group stage. A 3-1 win over Senegal followed by a 3-0 dismantling of Iraq produced scorelines that accurately reflected their underlying dominance. When a team's goals match or slightly exceed their xG, it means they're both creating quality chances and converting them at the expected rate. That's the hallmark of a title contender.

Argentina were similarly clinical. A 3-0 win over Algeria and a 2-0 victory against Austria produced a goal tally that matched their xG profile almost exactly. No waste, no luck — just ruthless efficiency. In tournament football, that combination is the most dangerous thing there is.

USA also deserve credit here. A 4-1 win over Paraguay and a 2-0 victory over Australia wasn't just about the results — the manner of those victories suggested a team creating high-quality chances and taking them. On home soil, with the crowd behind them, their xG profile makes them a genuine contender.

What This Means for the Knockouts

In tournament football, xG regression is real but it isn't guaranteed. A team that's been lucky can keep being lucky. A team that's been unlucky can keep converting at below their expected rate. But over enough games, the numbers tend to correct themselves.

The teams to worry about are those with a large positive gap between actual goals and xG — because that gap usually closes. The teams to back are those creating high xG and converting it efficiently, because that combination is sustainable.

France, Argentina and the USA fit that profile. They're not just winning — they're winning in a way the data supports. That matters enormously when games get tighter and margins shrink in the knockout rounds.

The xG data doesn't lie. It just takes a few games to make itself heard.
