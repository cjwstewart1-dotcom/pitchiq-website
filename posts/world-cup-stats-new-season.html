How the Tournament Stats Will Shape the New Season

July 2026 · 8 min read · PitchIQ

Every major tournament leaves a data trail. Analysts at every top club spent this summer watching, measuring and cataloguing — not just who won, but how they won, and what the underlying numbers said about performances the scorelines didn't fully capture. Here's what the data from this summer's tournament is going to mean for the 2026/27 Premier League season.

The High Press Is Back — And the Numbers Prove It

The most striking tactical trend of the tournament has been the return of the high press as the dominant defensive system. France, Argentina and the USA — the three teams whose xG profiles were most consistent — all deployed structured pressing systems that forced opponents into poor decisions high up the pitch. The data shows this clearly: the teams with the highest PPDA (passes allowed per defensive action, the primary metric for press intensity) were eliminated earlier than those with the lowest.

This will influence Premier League pre-seasons immediately. Clubs who have been running mid-block defensive systems will have compelling data to show their owners that pressing works at the highest level. Expect the intensity of pressing to increase across the top flight, and expect clubs to invest in players who can sustain it over 90 minutes — athletic, technically proficient forwards and midfielders who can chase down possession without losing their shape.

Set Pieces Are Statistically Undervalued — Not for Much Longer

One of the clearest data signals from the tournament has been the disproportionate impact of set pieces on outcomes. In the knockout rounds particularly, a significant proportion of decisive goals have come from corners, free kicks and throw-ins in dangerous areas. Yet xG models still systematically undervalue set pieces — partly because the data on defensive shape and aerial ability is harder to capture than open-play shot quality.

The clubs who will gain an edge in 2026/27 are those investing in set-piece coaching and analysis. This isn't a new insight — it's been available in the data for years — but the tournament has made it impossible to ignore. Expect the transfer market to reflect this, with clubs paying premiums for strong aerial presences in both penalty boxes and coaches with proven set-piece records.

What Germany's Exit Tells Every Premier League Manager

Germany's group stage trajectory — 7-1 against Curaçao, then consecutive failures against quality opposition — is a dataset every Premier League manager should study carefully. The lesson isn't that Germany are bad. It's that inflated aggregate numbers from weak opposition create false confidence, and that teams with high variance in performance are fundamentally more fragile in knockout-format competition.

For the Premier League, the equivalent is how clubs perform against the bottom half versus the top half. A team that accumulates xG comfortably against relegated fodder but shows a very different profile against the top eight is carrying hidden risk. The clubs that use their data teams to identify this pattern in opponents — and adjust their preparation accordingly — will have a measurable edge in the title race and European qualification places.

The xG Underperformers Are Transfer Targets

Some of the most valuable players in this summer's transfer window aren't the ones who scored the most. They're the ones whose underlying xG numbers were significantly higher than their actual output — players who created and took high-quality chances but were let down by slightly below-average finishing or goalkeeping performances.

This is where modern data analysis earns its money. A player who generated 6.8 xG across the tournament but only scored three goals isn't a bad finisher — they're likely a player whose conversion will regress towards the mean over a full Premier League season of 38 games. Clubs with strong analytics departments will identify these players before the market does. By the time the highlights-driven transfer media catches up, the price has already moved.

Possession Percentage Is Dead as a KPI

If the tournament has confirmed one statistical truth above all others, it's that possession percentage is a lagging, misleading metric when used as a primary performance indicator. Morocco conceded possession consistently and made the last 16. Several possession-heavy teams with technically superior squads went home early.

The shift in Premier League analytics is already underway — most top clubs abandoned raw possession as a target metric years ago — but the tournament has made this lesson visible to owners and boards who still cling to it. The clubs that are truly data-driven track xG differential, PPDA, progressive carries and shot quality. Possession is a byproduct of those things, not a cause of them. Any club still building their game model around the goal of keeping the ball needs to update their thinking before August.

The Big Takeaway for 2026/27

The tournament data points clearly in one direction: sustainable success at the highest level comes from teams that create high-quality chances, concede low-quality ones, and convert efficiently without relying on significant statistical variance. France, Argentina and the USA all fit that profile. Their tournament performances will be studied in training grounds across England and Scotland this summer.

For fans who want to track these metrics themselves as the new Premier League season unfolds, the numbers are no longer the preserve of club analysts. xG, shot quality, pressing intensity and possession-adjusted stats are available in real time. The clubs know this. The smart fans know it too.
