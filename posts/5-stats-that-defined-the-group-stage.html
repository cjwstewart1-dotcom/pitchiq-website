5 Stats That Defined the Group Stage

June 2026 · 6 min read · PitchIQ

Numbers don't lie — but they do need context. Here are the five statistics from the group stage that we keep coming back to, and what each one actually tells us about the tournament to come.

1. Canada 6-0 Qatar

Six. Zero. The largest margin of victory in the group stage, and a result that raised more questions than it answered. Canada were emphatic, clinical and relentless — and for about 72 hours, everyone was discussing them as potential dark horses. But look at the wider context: Qatar conceded heavily against multiple opponents throughout qualifying and their defensive organisation was among the weakest of any team at the tournament.

The stat matters not because of what it says about Canada, but because of what it says about the nature of group stage results. In a 48-team tournament, the weakest sides are dramatically weaker than in previous editions. That creates flattering scorelines and inflated metrics. The teams who thrive in the knockouts will be those whose numbers hold up against quality opposition — and we have plenty of evidence of how Canada perform against stronger sides. The 6-0 was spectacular. Treat it with appropriate scepticism.

2. Germany's 7-1 xG Paradox

Germany beat Curaçao 7-1. Their expected goals across the group stage were enormous. And yet they're out — eliminated by Paraguay on penalties after losing to Ecuador in the group. How?

The answer lies in aggregate vs. distribution. Germany's total xG was boosted enormously by the Curaçao performance, creating a misleading picture of their attacking quality across the tournament. When you weight xG by opponent quality — something bookmakers do and casual observers don't — Germany's underlying profile looks far less impressive. They couldn't replicate their output against teams who could actually press them back.

This is the xG paradox: a great total number can hide a wildly inconsistent performance pattern. Germany's group stage was effectively one elite performance and two mediocre ones. In tournament football, you only get one bad day before you're eliminated.

3. Sweden's 5 Goals in One Game — And Nobody Noticed

Sweden 5-1 Tunisia. An extraordinary attacking performance that generated almost no coverage outside Scandinavia. In a tournament full of dramatic results — Germany's exit, Morocco eliminating the Netherlands, Canada's six-goal opener — Sweden's emphatic win got buried in the noise.

But when you isolate the attacking output behind that result and look at Sweden's xG numbers across the group stage as a whole, a consistent pattern emerges: high-volume, high-quality chance creation with multiple goal contributors. That last point is crucial. Sweden aren't reliant on one player. Their goals have come from different sources, in different ways, against different defensive setups. That versatility is the single most predictive trait in tournament football. Sweden are criminally underrated.

4. Morocco's xG-Against: The Best Defensive Number in the Tournament

Morocco have conceded almost nothing in terms of xG across the tournament — not just goals conceded, but the quality of chances they've given away. Their defensive shape has been elite: low block, compact midfield, rapid transition to attack. They conceded one goal against the Netherlands in a match they ultimately won on penalties, and even that goal came from a set-piece — arguably the hardest type of goal to prevent purely through open-play defensive organisation.

Defensive xG-against is the least glamorous statistic in football and the most predictive in knockout tournament football. The team that doesn't concede wins. Morocco's numbers suggest they are the hardest team in the tournament to score against. That is worth significantly more than any attacking metric when games go to 90 minutes, extra time and penalties.

5. The Penalty Shootout Rate

Four shootouts have already occurred in this tournament — Netherlands vs Morocco, Germany vs Paraguay were the headline eliminations, plus two others in the bracket. That's an unusually high rate for this stage of a tournament, and it has real consequences for the teams who went through them.

Penalty shootouts are physically and psychologically demanding. Teams that have already been through one carry a small but statistically measurable disadvantage in subsequent games — not because of the pressure, but because of the additional playing time, the adrenaline crash, and the disruption to preparation schedules. Morocco beat the Netherlands on penalties and should be monitored closely for any performance dip in their next game. Conversely, the teams who won their group games comfortably — France, Mexico, Argentina — arrive in the knockouts fresher and with more psychological momentum. In a tournament where margins are fine, that edge is real.

The Takeaway

If there's one overarching lesson from the group stage statistics, it's this: ignore the outlier results. Germany's 7-1, Canada's 6-0, Sweden's 5-1 — these tell you something, but they tell you much less than you'd think about what happens next. The teams who go deep in tournament football are those whose numbers are consistently good, not spectacularly good once and then average. Consistency across three group games against varied opposition is the most reliable predictor of knockout success we have.

France, Argentina and the USA have that consistency. At least one of them will be in the final. The numbers are sure of it.
