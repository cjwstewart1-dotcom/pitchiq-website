WORLD CUP 2026

England and Scotland's chances of winning World Cup 2026 — what the data says

June 2026 · 7 min read · PitchIQ

Two British nations. One World Cup. Both carrying the hopes, dreams and entirely unrealistic expectations of their respective football-obsessed nations. We've cut through the emotion and looked at what the statistics actually tell us about England and Scotland's genuine prospects in North America.

Spoiler: one of these teams has a statistically credible route to the final. The other faces a rather steeper climb. We'll let you guess which is which.

England — finally matching the hype?

England's relationship with tournament football has been one of the great sporting tragedies of the modern era. The talent has often been there. The statistical profile has sometimes been there. But the results, at the crucial moments, haven't followed.

So what do the numbers say about England in 2026?

The defensive data is encouraging

England's most significant statistical improvement over recent years has been defensive. Their xG against average of 0.72 per match across competitive internationals puts them in the top five defensively among all World Cup qualifiers. The defensive shape is organised, the pressing triggers are consistent and the goalkeeper situation — historically a concern — is no longer a weakness.

📊 England's xG against has improved from 1.1 per match in 2021 to 0.72 in their most recent qualifying campaign — a 35% defensive improvement reflected in their results.

The attacking data is mixed

Here's where England's statistics get more complicated. Their xG for average of 1.89 per match is good but not elite. More concerning is the gap between xG and actual goals scored — England consistently underperform their xG, meaning they're creating better chances than they're converting.

Over the last 20 competitive matches, England have scored 0.31 goals fewer per game than their xG suggested they should. That's a meaningful gap. It suggests either a finishing problem, a problem with the quality of xG model being applied, or simply the kind of statistical variance that corrects itself over time. In a knockout tournament, variance can be fatal.

The pressing profile

England's PPDA of 9.1 is solid without being exceptional. They press with organisation but not with the relentless intensity of Spain (6.8) or France (7.2). This is partly a tactical choice — England under recent management have prioritised defensive shape over high pressing — but it means they concede more of the ball in their own half than the very best teams.

England's realistic route

A Poisson model run on England's statistical profile gives them approximately a 12-15% probability of winning the tournament. That makes them genuine contenders — third or fourth favourites — but with a significant gap to France and Spain at the top.

Their most likely route to the final runs through a draw that avoids France and Brazil until the semi-finals. Their xG profile suggests they're capable of beating any team on their day. The question, as always, is whether they can do it consistently across seven matches.

📊 Historical note: since the introduction of xG modelling, no team with an xG differential below +0.8 per match has won a World Cup. England's +1.17 clears that bar comfortably.

Scotland — the romantic underdog story

Scotland's presence at a World Cup is itself a statistical outlier. Their qualification, achieved through a combination of tactical discipline, collective effort and the kind of defiant spirit that has characterised Scottish football for generations, was not something the pre-campaign xG models would have predicted as likely.

That tells you something important about Scotland: they've already exceeded their statistical expectations once. Can they do it again on the biggest stage of all?

What the numbers show

Scotland's qualifying statistics reveal a team that is extremely hard to beat but limited in their ability to impose themselves on higher-quality opponents. Their xG against of 0.81 per match is respectable — they're defensively organised and difficult to break down. Their xG for of 1.24 per match tells the honest story of a team that creates chances without generating the volume or quality of the top sides.

Scotland's xG differential of +0.43 per match is positive but modest. It reflects a team that is competitive against comparable opposition but will face a significant statistical deficit against the tournament's elite.

📊 Scotland stats (qualifying): xG for: 1.24 | xG against: 0.81 | xG differential: +0.43 | Pass accuracy: 81% | PPDA: 11.2

The group stage opportunity

The expanded 48-team format is, statistically, Scotland's greatest ally. With 32 teams advancing from the group stage — including the eight best third-placed teams — Scotland don't need to dominate their group. They need to accumulate enough points to progress, then take it one match at a time.

In knockout football, a single match xG model gives any team a non-trivial probability of victory. Scotland's defensive organisation means they're capable of keeping any match tight. And tight matches — as Greece proved at Euro 2004 and Iceland proved at Euro 2016 — can go either way regardless of the underlying statistics.

Where Scotland can hurt teams

Scotland's most effective attacking pattern in qualifying was transition football — winning the ball high and exploiting the space behind opposition defensive lines before they could reorganise. Their progressive carry rate is higher than their possession numbers suggest, indicating they're effective at moving the ball quickly in dangerous situations.

Set pieces are also statistically significant. Scotland rank in the top 10 of all World Cup qualifiers for xG generated from corners and free kicks. In knockout football, where margins are smallest, that can be decisive.

Scotland's realistic route

A Poisson model gives Scotland approximately a 2-3% probability of winning the tournament. That's not zero — and it's higher than it would have been for many previous Scotland squads. A quarter-final appearance would constitute an overperformance relative to their statistical profile but would not be an impossibility given the expanded format and the unpredictability of knockout football.

📊 Scotland's best statistical outcome: progress from the group stage, face a beatable Round of 32 opponent, and take it from there. Their defensive profile gives them a puncher's chance in any single match.

Head to head — the numbers compared

If England and Scotland were to meet in this tournament (theoretically possible in the knockout rounds), what would the data suggest?

England's xG differential advantage of +0.74 per match over Scotland is significant. England create more and concede less. Their passing quality is higher, their pressing is more effective and their squad depth is substantially greater. The statistical verdict would favour England comfortably.

But football, infuriatingly, doesn't always follow the statistical verdict. Scotland vs England is never just a football match. And history has shown that the numbers matter less when national pride, a sold-out stadium and ninety minutes of nerve-shredding football are involved.

The verdict

England: Statistically credible tournament contenders. The data supports genuine optimism — not the manufactured, traditional optimism that evaporates at the first setback, but numbers-backed confidence that this squad can beat anyone. The conversion problem needs to resolve itself. If it does, a semi-final is a realistic statistical outcome.

Scotland: Long shots who have already beaten the odds once by qualifying. Their defensive organisation gives them a chance in any individual match. Tournament football rewards the teams that are hard to beat — and Scotland are very hard to beat. Progress from the group stage and you never know what happens next.

Either way, both sets of fans are in for a ride. The data can predict the probabilities. It can't predict the drama.
